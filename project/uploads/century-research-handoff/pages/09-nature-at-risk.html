The measures

Nineteen entries in six systems. Each gives the figure, how it was measured and how solid it is.

Species and populations 6 entries

  1. 2019Assessment

    A million species threatened

    The IPBES global assessment found that around 1 million animal and plant species are now threatened with extinction. It put the threatened share at more than 40 percent of amphibians, almost 33 percent of reef-forming corals and more than a third of marine mammals, with a tentative 10 percent for insects. The average abundance of native species in most major land-based habitats has fallen by at least 20 percent.

    Drivers
    Ranked by global impact: changes in land and sea use, direct exploitation of organisms, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species.

    How solid A UN summary of the 2019 assessment. The insect figure is labelled tentative, and the page gives no share for all species.

  2. 2023 / 2026Red List

    Share of species groups threatened

    The IUCN Red List, in version 2023-1, puts the best-estimate threatened share at 41 percent of amphibians, 26 percent of mammals, 12 percent of birds, 36 percent of reef-forming corals, 70 percent of cycads and 34 percent of conifers. A magazine summary of the 2026 update reports 175,909 species on the list, of which 49,505 are threatened.

    Ranges
    Amphibians 36–47 percent, mammals 23–37, birds 12–13, reef corals 31–44, conifers 34–35.

    How solid The 2023 percentages come from the IUCN page, which gives ranges. The 2026 counts come from a magazine summary, not from IUCN, and I did not see the release date.

  3. 1970–2022Index

    Wildlife populations down 73 percent on average

    The Living Planet Report 2026 tracks 35,803 populations of 5,790 species of mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish. Their average decline was 73 percent from 1970 to 2022, about 2.5 percent a year.

    By region
    South America 95 percent, Africa 80 percent.
    By habitat
    Freshwater 85 percent, land 69 percent, marine 59 percent.
    Threats
    Habitat loss and degradation were reported most often, followed by overexploitation, climate change, pollution, invasive species and disease.

    How solid The report itself says the figure is not a headcount of animals lost and is especially sensitive to changes in already small populations. The 2026 edition added 295 species and 967 populations to the 2024 data.

  4. 2018Estimate

    Livestock outweigh wild animals

    Our World in Data, summarizing a 2018 study in PNAS, gives humans 0.01 percent of total biomass and 2.5 percent of animal biomass. Livestock account for 4 percent of animal biomass, more than 50 percent above all humans together. Wild mammals and birds together account for 0.38 percent, so livestock outweigh them by a factor of ten.

    Poultry
    About 0.2 percent of animal biomass, more than twice that of wild birds.

    How solid An estimate of mass, not of numbers. The page gives no figure for how much wild mammal biomass has changed over time, so it shows the present balance only.

  5. 2023Assessment

    Invasive species

    IPBES reports that 37,000 alien species have established worldwide and that 3,500 have become problematic invasives. The page says invasive alien species are the sole cause of 16 percent of extinctions and a major driver of 60 percent, and that they are responsible for 90 percent of extinctions on islands.

    Cost
    A 2019 IPBES estimate of $423 billion. The cost of impacts has more than quadrupled every decade since 1970, according to the page.

    How solid Read through an Australian biodiversity council's summary of the report, not IPBES's own page. The page does not say whether the $423 billion is a yearly figure.

  6. 1925–2018Surveys

    Insects

    A 2017 study of flying insect biomass at 63 German nature reserves, measured from 1989, found an average fall of 76 percent over 27 years, and 82 percent in summer. A 2020 meta-analysis in Science of 166 long-term surveys at 1,676 sites worldwide, covering 1925 to 2018, found land insect abundance falling by an average of 0.92 percent a year, about 24 percent over 30 years.

    Contrast
    The same meta-analysis found freshwater insect abundance rising by an average of 1.08 percent a year, about 38 percent over 30 years, which the authors link possibly to water protection policies.
    Where
    Land declines were strongest in parts of the United States and in Europe, especially Germany, with the steepest European declines since 2005. Tree-canopy insects were largely unchanged.
    Cause
    The German study did not identify one; pesticides were a suggested explanation. The meta-analysis associates land declines with habitat destruction, particularly urbanization.

    How solid The 76 percent figure comes from a news report whose text gave no publication year (its web address is dated October 2017) and that says only that many reserves border farmland. The meta-analysis measures abundance, not species, and says local trends vary widely, with weaker trends in less disturbed areas described as tentative.

Land and forests 3 entries

  1. 2019 / 2022Assessment

    Land altered and degraded

    IPBES found that three-quarters of the land-based environment and about 66 percent of the marine environment have been significantly altered by human actions, and that land degradation has cut the productivity of 23 percent of the land surface. A UNCCD report, as covered by EcoWatch, says human activity has degraded as much as 40 percent of the world's land.

    Restoration
    The UNCCD scenario calls for five billion hectares to be restored, which the article puts at 35 percent of Earth's land area.
    Economy
    The article says land degradation puts about half of global GDP at risk.

    How solid The 23 percent and the up-to-40 percent use different definitions and sources, so they do not conflict. The UNCCD figures reached me through a news article, and the GDP claim is the article's.

  2. 2025Satellite record

    Tropical forest and fire

    Global Forest Watch reports that the world lost 4.3 million hectares of tropical primary forest in 2025. That was 36 percent less than in 2024, and still 46 percent higher than a decade ago.

    All forests
    Global tree cover loss was 25.5 million hectares in 2025, and fires caused 42 percent of it.

    How solid WRI's own summary of Global Forest Watch data. The 42 percent fire share applies to all tree cover loss, not to tropical primary forest alone.

  3. 2015 / 2023Assessment

    Soils

    FAO's 2015 Status of the World's Soil Resources reports that 33 percent of land is moderately to highly degraded through erosion, salinization, compaction, acidification and chemical pollution, and that conditions are worsening in far more cases than they are improving. Erosion removes 25 to 40 billion tonnes of topsoil each year.

    Crops
    Cereal losses from erosion are estimated at 7.6 million tonnes a year, and could exceed 253 million tonnes by 2050 without action. Salinization affects an estimated 760,000 square kilometres.
    Life
    A 2023 study led by Mark Anthony, in PNAS, estimates that 59 percent of all species depend on soil, including 90 percent of fungi, 86 percent of plants and 40 percent of bacteria.
    Reversible
    FAO says the decline can be reversed if countries promote sustainable soil management.

    How solid The FAO figures are from its own 2015 press release and are a decade old. The 59 percent is a synthesis of earlier estimates, not a census, and the page I read gives no margin of error.

Fresh water and wetlands 1 entry

  1. 1970–2025Assessment

    Wetlands

    The Global Wetland Outlook 2025 reports a loss of more than 4 million square kilometres of wetland since 1970 across 11 wetland types, a 22 percent fall in extent. About a quarter of the remaining wetlands are degraded and are being destroyed at 0.52 percent a year.

    Dependence
    Roughly 40 percent of the world's plant and animal species rely on wetlands at some point in their lives.
    Value
    The remaining 14 million square kilometres provide $8 trillion to $39 trillion of ecosystem services a year.
    Outlook
    Up to 20 percent of the world's wetlands could be lost in the next 25 years.

    How solid Read through a Mongabay summary. The value range is wide because the figure depends on how services are priced, and the forecast is the report's, not mine.

Oceans and reefs 3 entries

  1. 2024Assessment

    Fish stocks

    The FAO's State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture 2026 puts the share of sustainably fished stocks at 62.4 percent, which leaves 37.6 percent overfished. Wild catch was stable at about 92 million tonnes in 2024, inside the 86 to 94 million tonne band it has held since the late 1980s, while farmed aquatic animal production passed 100 million tonnes for the first time.

    Change
    The sustainable share fell 2.1 points from the 2024 report.

    How solid Read through Mongabay. The number of assessed stocks rose from about 500 to 2,665, mostly because stocks were split into separate assessments, so comparison with earlier editions is harder.

  2. 2023–2025Satellite and survey

    Coral bleaching

    NOAA confirmed the fourth global coral bleaching event on 15 April 2024. Bleaching-level heat stress ran from early 2023 to mid-2025 and reached 84 percent of the world's coral reef area. Mass bleaching was documented in at least 83 countries and territories across the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian oceans.

    Tipping point
    The 2025 Global Tipping Points report says warm-water coral reefs are now passing their tipping point, with widespread dieback under way. Reefs support nearly a billion people and a quarter of all marine life.

    How solid NOAA's own page says the event likely concluded in mid-2025 and that it was waiting on the December 2025 to February 2026 summer to be sure; my read predates that check. The tipping-point statement comes from a Stockholm Resilience Centre summary that gives no temperature threshold.

  3. 2026Red List

    Deep-sea habitats

    A magazine summary of the IUCN Red List 2026 update says about 62 percent of endemic hydrothermal vent molluscs, 125 of 201 species, are threatened by deep-sea mining. It names a deep-sea snail from the Indian Ocean, Lirapex felix, listed as Critically Endangered because of mining exploration.

    How solid A secondary summary whose excerpt cut off, so other changes in the update may exist. No commercial mining has been approved; see the chapter on defenses.

Ice and sea level 2 entries

  1. 1975–2025Monitoring

    Glaciers

    The World Glacier Monitoring Service reports net mass loss of 408 gigatonnes in the 2025 hydrological year for glaciers outside the ice sheets, sixth-most negative on record, equal to 1.1 millimetres of sea level. Since 1975 the cumulative loss is 9,583 gigatonnes, equal to 26.4 millimetres.

    Rate
    Annual loss rose from under 100 gigatonnes in 1976–1995 to about 230 in 1996–2015 and about 390 in 2016–2025.
    Recent
    Six of the highest mass-loss years on record fell in the past seven years.

    How solid WGMS's own report. Each figure carries an uncertainty (408 ± 132 Gt for 2025), and the page gives no share of ice lost.

  2. 1993–2025Satellite record

    Sea level

    NASA reports that average global sea level has risen about 10 centimetres since 1993, and that the annual rate of rise has more than doubled over three decades. In 2025 the rise was about 0.8 millimetres, down from about 5.9 millimetres in 2024.

    How solid NASA's own page. The 2025 figure is one year and swings with ocean cycles such as La Niña; the trend over three decades is the point. I converted inches and centimetres to millimetres.

Chemistry, waste and Earth systems 4 entries

  1. 1950–2019Estimate

    Plastic

    World plastic production rose nearly 230-fold, from 2 million tonnes in 1950 to 460 million tonnes in 2019. Roughly 350 million tonnes of plastic waste are generated each year, and about a quarter is mismanaged.

    Ocean
    Between 1 and 2 million tonnes a year are estimated to enter the oceans, about 0.5 percent of waste generated. Earlier estimates ran as high as 8 million.

    How solid Our World in Data's summary of OECD and academic estimates. The page says the uncertainties are large, and the ocean figure has been revised downward.

  2. 1900–2023Estimate

    Nitrogen

    Our World in Data estimates that around half of the global population depends on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, within a range of 40 to 50 percent. Without it the population may have been only about 4 billion, against over 8 billion in 2023.

    Link
    The 2026 Planetary Health Check lists modification of biogeochemical flows, which covers nitrogen, among the transgressed boundaries. The connection between the two entries is mine.

    How solid The fertilizer share is hard to derive with confidence, because breeding, irrigation and mechanization also raised yields.

  3. 2026Assessment

    Planetary boundaries

    The 2026 Planetary Health Check finds that seven of nine boundaries are transgressed: climate change, biosphere integrity, land system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows, novel entities and ocean acidification. All seven are at their highest recorded levels of transgression. Ocean acidification was the most recent to be breached, in 2025.

    Not breached
    Stratospheric ozone depletion and atmospheric aerosol loading, both improved over the last decade, though the authors caution that global progress may mask unsafe aerosol levels in some regions.

    How solid Read through a summary posted on Astrobiology.com. The boundaries are a scientific framework, not a measured threshold at which collapse begins.

  4. 1958–2026Measurement

    Carbon dioxide and temperature

    Carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa rose from 313 parts per million in 1958 to a May 2026 peak of 432 parts per million. The WMO puts 2025 at 1.43 °C above the pre-industrial level, with an uncertainty of 0.13 °C.

    How solid Both figures were read for the chapter on vulnerability, from a Japanese research-centre page citing the Mauna Loa record and from a trade-press report of the WMO statement. Neither is the original release.

What stands out

These are my reading of the entries, not findings from the sources.

Reach

Three-quarters of the land environment and two-thirds of the marine environment are significantly altered, and the 84 percent of reef area hit by heat stress shows that even remote ecosystems are affected.

Scale

Plastic output grew about 230-fold from 1950 to 2019, carbon dioxide rose from 313 to 432 parts per million, and the yearly rate of glacier loss is roughly four times its 1976–1995 level.

Speed

Monitored wildlife populations fell by an average of 73 percent in 52 years. The fourth global bleaching event reached 84 percent of reef area in about two and a half years.

Dependence

Reefs support nearly a billion people, wetlands about 40 percent of species, and synthetic nitrogen about half of humanity, so pressure on these systems is linked to everything that depends on them.

No clock for recovery

Several entries describe states, not flows: a reef tipping point said to be passing, seven boundaries at their highest recorded levels, and glaciers that lose ice faster each period.

What this chapter cannot show

  • Measured records are short. The Living Planet index starts in 1970 and the satellite and bleaching records are far shorter, so the chapter cannot compare today with earlier centuries.
  • The measures are not comparable. The charts mix population indices, shares of area and shares of stocks, and the labels say which is which.
  • Indices have known weaknesses. The Living Planet figure is an average that is sensitive to small populations, and the Red List shares carry wide ranges.
  • Not covered: groundwater, air pollution's effect on ecosystems, noise, and the many local losses that never reach a global index. The insect and soil entries are thinner than the others, resting on few studies and, for soils, a 2015 report.
  • Most figures reached me through summaries, so they need checking against the original reports before use.

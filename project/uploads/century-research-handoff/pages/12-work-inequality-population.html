How people live

Ten entries in three groups. Each gives the scale, what the source says about change and how solid the figures are.

Work 4 entries

  1. TodaySectors

    From the farm to other work

    Around one-quarter of the world's labour force works in agriculture. In many low- and middle-income countries the majority works in farming and relies on it as its main income, as most people did in rich countries a few centuries ago. As countries get richer the share working in agriculture tends to fall as people move into industry and services.

    Productivity
    Value added per farm worker can differ ten-fold between countries.

    How solid The page gives the one-quarter figure and these statements, but its history is in charts whose data the text does not include. I have no figure for the share in agriculture in 1825 or 1925.

  2. 2000 to 2024Child labour

    Children at work

    The ILO and UNICEF estimate nearly 138 million children were in child labour in 2024, down from 246 million in 2000, with about 54 million in hazardous work. Agriculture accounts for 61%, services 27% and industry 13%. Sub-Saharan Africa has about 87 million, nearly two-thirds of the total, while in Asia and the Pacific prevalence fell from 6% to 3% since 2020.

    History
    Child work was widespread in 19th-century Europe and North America and fell rapidly around the turn of the 20th century. Our World in Data says England's 1900 child labour rate was similar to today's global rate. Among 12 to 14 year olds in England and Wales it fell to about 6–7% after the First World War and rose to 15.3% by 1944.
    Risk
    The release warns that global funding cuts threaten to roll back the gains.

    How solid The 2024 and 2000 figures are from the ILO and UNICEF release. Our World in Data gives 265 million in 2013 from an earlier ILO report, and the two reports may not use the same method, so I do not draw a straight line through them.

  3. 1990 to 2024Women's work

    Women in paid work

    Around half of women worldwide take part in the labour force, a figure ILO estimates say has stayed around 50% since the early 1990s, while men's participation fell from 80% in 1990 to around 73%. In 2024 it was 62% in Sweden, 56% in Germany and 41% in Italy.

    Unpaid work
    Women spend more time on unpaid care than men in every country, in some by more than six times. Average US household housework fell from 58 hours a week in 1900 to 18 in 1975.

    How solid From Our World in Data's page on the ILO's modelled estimates. The page gives no regional percentages and no US participation figures for 1900 or 1950.

  4. 2026Informal work

    Work outside formal protection

    An ILO analysis reported on 12 May 2026 found nearly 58% of workers globally in informal jobs.

    How solid Thin. It is a news report of an ILO analysis, with no worker count, no regions and no definition, and I did not read the ILO's own page.

Inequality 2 entries

  1. TodayBetween countries

    How much depends on where you live

    Milanovic (2015) is cited as finding that the country a person lives in explains two-thirds of the variation of income differences among all people. The poorer half of the world, almost 4 billion people, live on less than $6.70 a day, and someone on $30 a day is in the richest 15%. Swedes are about 30 times richer than in 1820, and about 96% of the world's people live in the country they were born in.

    Prices
    The figures use 2011 international dollars from an earlier World Bank release, and the page notes the World Bank has since updated its data.

    How solid From Our World in Data's introduction to global inequality. The page gives no Gini or Theil values and no top-10% shares, so I cannot show how total inequality moved over two centuries.

  2. 2025Shares of income and wealth

    Who holds what, now

    The World Inequality Report 2026, as reported, says the richest 10% earn 53% of global income and the poorer half of adults, about 2.8 billion, 8%. The richest 10% own 75% of global wealth, the richest 1% own 37% and the poorer half 2%. Around 56,000 people, the top 0.001%, hold more wealth than 2.8 billion adults together.

    Regions
    Average wealth in North America and Oceania in 2025 is more than sixteen times that of Sub-Saharan Africa.

    How solid From a news report of the report, not the report. It gives no earlier shares, so it shows how things stand and not whether they have changed.

Population and where people live 4 entries

  1. 1963 to 2086Growth

    The peak of population

    Population growth peaked in the 1960s at over 2% a year, 2.3% in 1963 on the chart, and was 0.9% in 2023. The UN projects a peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and about 10.2 billion by 2100, from 8.2 billion in 2024. 63 countries have already passed their peak, including China, Germany, Japan and Russia. The number of children under five peaked in 2017.

    Versions
    Our World in Data's text says the peak is in 2086 at just over 10.4 billion, while a chart on the same page says about 10.3 billion in 2084, and the two seem to come from different UN editions.

    How solid These are projections and the page says they are sensitive to assumptions about fertility.

  2. 1950s to 2023Fertility

    Fewer children per woman

    The world's fertility rate was 4.9 in the 1950s and 2.3 in 2023. Niger had 6 in 2023. South Korea fell from around 6 in the 1950s to less than 1, and most countries in East Asia were below 1. India fell from about 5 in the 1970s to about 2. More than half of all countries are below the replacement level of 2.1, and nearly a fifth have fewer than 1.4.

    How solid Our World in Data and the UN, read through a summarizing tool. The UN page gave no world fertility rate for 2024 and none for 1950 itself, so the 1950s figure is Our World in Data's.

  3. Late 2070sAgeing

    More old people than children

    The UN expects people aged 65 or over to outnumber children under 18 by the late 2070s. Global life expectancy was 73.3 in 2024, up from 70.9 in the pandemic.

    How solid A projection. The page gave no share of the population aged 65 or over today, and its pandemic life expectancy of 70.9 differs from the 71.5 that WHO gives in Chapter 3, and I did not check why.

  4. TodayCrowding

    Cities and slums

    Around one in four people in urban areas live in slum households, and in sub-Saharan Africa more than half of urban residents do, with Chad near 80%.

    Link
    Chapter 1 gives the rise in the share of people living in cities.

    How solid From Our World in Data's page on urbanization. It gives no total count of people living in slums and no year for the shares.

What the record shows

These are my reading of the entries and my arithmetic, not findings from the sources.

Where people live now matters more than much else

The country of residence explains two-thirds of income differences, 96% of people live where they were born, and child labour, informal work, slum living and low internet access all concentrate in the poorer regions, on the figures in this and the earlier chapters.

Child labour has fallen by almost half in 24 years and is still large

From 246 million in 2000 to 138 million in 2024 is a fall of 44%, on my division, and the ILO release says funding cuts could reverse it.

The turn in population is already here for many countries

More than half of countries are below replacement fertility and 63 have passed their peak, while the world total is projected to keep rising until the 2080s.

Shares of income and wealth are given for one year only

The 2025 figures show the shape of inequality but not its history, so this chapter cannot say whether inequality rose or fell across the century.

The data do not fall on 1825 and 1925

Child labour starts in 2000, fertility in the 1950s, inequality shares in 2025. A comparison of the two centuries cannot be made on these figures.

What this chapter cannot show

  • Almost no series here reaches back to 1825 or 1925. The chapter describes the present and the last seven decades.
  • Several figures are news reports of the World Inequality Report and ILO analyses. I did not read the reports themselves.
  • The child labour totals for 2000 and 2013 come from different reports and may not be on the same footing.
  • Informal work, women's work and slum living are given with few numbers and no earlier dates.
  • Population projections depend on assumptions about fertility and differ between UN editions.
  • Pay, hours and working conditions in rich countries are covered only in Chapter 4, and not here.

Chapter 16 · Weighing

The comparison

The hypothesis stated, then tested six ways against the earlier chapters

The earlier chapters do not show the worst losses in this century. They do show exposures at the scale of the whole world that none of my 19 selected earlier events matches. This chapter sets out what supports and what weakens the hypothesis.

Built from Chapters 1 to 15, whose figures are current to between 13 and 27 October 2026. No new research.

The hypothesis

The hypothesis

Humanity has made itself vulnerable to catastrophic outcomes through the last 100 years of advancement, unlike any other 100 years of advancement in known history.

This is the project's starting hypothesis. No earlier chapter stated it. They gathered evidence on one side or the other, and this chapter puts the evidence side by side.

The hypothesis has parts that can be checked separately. A century that was uniquely dangerous should show at least some of these: the largest losses on record, the largest possible losses, the widest reach, harms that are hardest to undo, the heaviest dependence on a few systems, and defenses that have not kept up. Each test below states what it expects, sets out what the earlier chapters recorded for and against, and gives a result. The results are my judgment, not findings from the sources.

The tests

Six tests and two problems with testing. Each gives what the chapters recorded and how solid it is.

Six tests of the hypothesis 6 entries

  1. Test 1Not supported

    Realised loss: the worst losses came earlier

    If this century were uniquely dangerous, it should hold the heaviest losses on record. Chapter 7 does not show that. It puts the Black Death at 30–60% of Europe's people and the post-1492 collapse at 80–90% of the Indigenous population of the Americas. The largest share losses in the last 100 years among its entries are the Second World War at 3.0–3.7% of the world and the Bengal famine at 1.3–6.3% of Bengal. COVID-19 is about 0.2% of the world.

    In headcount
    The Second World War's 60 to 85 million deaths are above the usual Black Death range of 25 to 50 million and below the higher estimates of 75 to 200 million. The world has more people now, so a headcount compares less well than a share.
    Falling toll
    Chapter 8 reports that disaster death rates fell by more than 90% over the century and that recent famines have been far less deadly than earlier ones.
    What this does
    It does not refute the hypothesis, which is about exposure. It does show that the hypothesis cannot rest on what has already happened.

    How solid Share figures use different bases, a region, a city, a country and the world, as the labels say. Many ranges are wide: the Black Death differs by up to a factor of eight. Most of Chapter 7 comes from Wikipedia.

  2. Test 2Supported as a model result only

    Size of the worst case: potentially as large as the largest past loss

    Chapter 6 reports a 2022 Nature Food study that finds more than 2 billion possible famine deaths after an India–Pakistan war and more than 5 billion after a US–Russia war. Against today's 8.2 billion people that is about 24% and 61%, which is close to the Black Death's 30–60% of Europe and below the Americas' 80–90%. Those earlier losses were regional. This scenario is global.

    Arsenals
    Chapter 6 gives 12,187 warheads in January 2026, about 2,100 to 2,200 of them on high alert. The chapter on defenses records that the total is about 82–83% below its 1986 peak of 70,300.
    Treaty and clock
    New START expired on 5 February 2026, and the Doomsday Clock stands at 85 seconds, the closest ever.
    Close calls
    The 1983 Petrov false alarm and the 1962 submarine B-59 account are both in Chapter 6. The second is debated by historians.

    How solid A modelled scenario that the chapter says shows scale and is not a forecast. I divided by 8.2 billion myself, and Chapter 6 does not say what population base the study used. Nothing like this has happened, so the evidence is a calculation and not an observed event.

  3. Test 3Supported in kind, not in novelty

    Reach: human-made channels now span the world

    Chapter 7 finds that only a few events reached across the world: the 1918 influenza, smallpox, COVID-19 and the two world wars. The rest struck a region, a continent or an empire. Chapter 6 lists exposures that work at planetary scale by their nature: the atmosphere, the oceans, global supply chains and global travel. Carbon dioxide rose from 313 to 432 ppm between 1958 and May 2026, and seven of nine planetary boundaries are transgressed together.

    Against novelty
    Disease crossed regions on the transport of its day in 541, 1346 and 1918 (Chapter 7). Chapter 10 records natural events with global reach in deep time, such as the asteroid strike that removed about 75% of species 66 million years ago. Global-scale harm is old.
    Against, on speed
    Chapter 2 shows Atlantic crossing times falling by factors of about 8 and then about 25 in two stages (its arithmetic), so the speed of connection has risen. It does not show that the speed changed the size of any harm.

    How solid The counts and measurements are solid, since carbon dioxide is measured directly. The step from them to catastrophe is a judgment, and the boundary framework is a scientific proposal and not a settled threshold, as Chapter 6 says.

  4. Test 4Mixed

    Reversibility: some harms have no recovery clock

    Past human catastrophes recovered, slowly. Chapter 7 reports that Europe regained its pre-plague population in the 16th century, Ireland's decline lasted a century, and the cooling after Tambora was temporary. Chapter 9 describes several present changes as states and not flows: a coral reef tipping point said to be passing, seven boundaries at their highest recorded levels, and glaciers losing ice faster each period. Chapter 10 puts recovery from the great extinctions at about 6 to 75 million years.

    Against
    Chapter 11 records a humpback whale population back to about 93% of its pre-whaling size roughly 33 years after the ban, and Chapter 8 records the ozone layer expected to return to 1980 levels between 2040 and 2066. Some harms did reverse.
    A caution
    A 73% average fall in monitored wildlife populations over 52 years is not a count of extinctions, and the source says so. Chapter 10 puts the main End-Permian marine pulse at 60,000 to 100,000 years, so the two are not like for like.
    Hypothetical
    Chapter 6 also records a warning that mirror-image organisms might cause irreversible harm. They do not exist.

    How solid The living-world figures are measured averages with wide caveats. Recovery times for deep-time events are estimates, and the comparison with today's rates is mine.

  5. Test 5Cannot be compared on these figures

    Dependence and margins: more people rest on fewer systems

    Chapter 6 gives two sharp examples. About 40 to 50% of the world's food depends on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, and without it the world might hold around 4 billion people, not over 8 billion. About 92% of the most advanced logic chips are made in Taiwan. Chapter 12 puts world population at 8.2 billion in 2024, so any single failure now falls on more people than at any time before.

    Earlier dependence
    Chapter 7 records that Ireland in the 1840s depended on a single crop and was struck by blight. Concentrated dependence is not new, though the Irish case was regional.
    What is missing
    The chapters give no figure for how much of the world depended on any single system in 1825 or 1925, so I cannot say it has increased.

    How solid The two present-day figures are secondhand estimates, the fertilizer one from a single published estimate and the chip share from a news piece citing a research group. Nothing comparable exists in the earlier chapters, so this test stays open.

  6. Test 6Mixed

    Defenses: large gains on known hazards, little yet on the newest

    Chapter 8 records large measured falls where the hazard is known and countable: smallpox eradicated, wild polio cases down by more than 99% since 1988, child deaths before 15 down by about 84% from 1950 to 2020, disaster death rates down by more than 90%. It also records weakening: New START expired, the United States left the WHO on 22 January 2026, the pandemic agreement's access annex was unfinished, UNEP projects 2.8 °C of warming under current policies, and about 38% of near-Earth asteroids over 140 metres were known in 2023.

    The living world
    Chapter 11 records 17.6% of land and 8.4% of ocean protected against a 30% target for each, and fewer than half of aligned national targets on track.
    The newest technology
    For AI and engineered pathogens, Chapter 8 describes a safety report, an EU law and a convention, and says it found no figures showing a fall in harm from any of them.

    How solid Cause is hard to show: a fall in deaths may come from the defense, from wealth or from both. Several figures are secondhand, and the chapter marks the 154 million lives saved by vaccination as a model.

Two problems with the test itself 2 entries

  1. Problem 1Cannot be tested as worded

    “Unlike any other 100 years” needs a century-by-century comparison

    The hypothesis claims that no other century, in all of known history, matches this one. My record cannot test that. Chapter 7 is 19 events I selected, not a tally of every century. It is thinner for events outside Europe, East Asia and the Middle East, and a record kept by survivors lists only the catastrophes that were survived. No other 100 years in the chapters has a measured exposure that can be set against the figures in Chapter 6.

    What can be said
    Among the 19 events, none matches the modelled scenario in Test 2 for reach. That finding is limited to a list I chose.
    Survivors
    A record of survived catastrophes also cannot show what was narrowly avoided in earlier centuries.

    How solid A limit of the evidence, stated in Chapter 7 itself.

  2. Problem 2Cannot be netted

    The gains and the exposure are different kinds of quantity

    The earlier chapters also record large gains, set out in the ledger below. Deaths averted, years of life and meals supplied are counted outcomes. The exposure in Chapter 6 is mostly a modelled or judged possibility. They cannot be added into one balance, and the hypothesis does not claim they should be. It says the century is more exposed, which is compatible with the century also being healthier and richer.

    Shared causes
    The same fertilizer that feeds about half the world is also a main driver of the nitrogen boundary being breached (Chapter 6).
    Expert estimates differ
    For extinction from AI by 2100, domain experts gave 3% and superforecasters 0.38% in the 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, while Toby Ord gives 1 in 10 for this century. Ord calls his figures order-of-magnitude indications.

    How solid Probability figures are opinions, not measurements, and the tournament's 3% is about 8 times its 0.38%, and Ord's 1 in 10 is about 26 times the lower figure. Those ratios are my arithmetic.

What the weighing shows

These are my reading of the tests, not findings from the sources.

On what has happened, this is not the worst century

In share terms the largest losses in my record came earlier. No event in the last 100 years among my entries matches the 30–60% regional losses; the highest share is Bengal's 6.3% at the top of its range.

On what could happen, the record shows something new in reach

The modelled worst case is global, and it comes from choices people make. Among the 19 events I selected, none has that combination.

The two kinds of evidence differ in strength

Realised losses are counted, with wide ranges. The worst cases are modelled or judged, and expert probabilities differ widely. Weight them accordingly.

Defenses and exposure grew from the same capacity

Fertilizer, medicine, forecasting and trade raised living standards and also built the dependence in Chapter 6. This is my reading of the chapters, not a finding.

A narrower claim fits the evidence better than the wording does

The chapters do not show a century unlike any other in every respect. They do support this narrower form: this is the first century in my record in which human choices carry measured or modelled effects at the scale of the whole world. That is my judgment, and the hypothesis may still be worth keeping in its original form.

What this chapter cannot show

  • This chapter uses only the earlier chapters and adds no new research. Their limits carry over, including secondhand figures and, for Chapter 7, mostly Wikipedia.
  • The results are my judgment, labelled as such. Another reader could grade the same evidence differently.
  • Chapters 12 to 15 rarely reach back to 1925, so for those the two centuries cannot be compared. Chapter 13 gives no verdict at all.
  • The share figures use different bases. A region, a city, a country and the world are not the same denominator.
  • Modelled scenarios and expert probabilities are not observations. I included only the figures the earlier chapters recorded.
  • The chapters are dated between 13 and 27 October 2026. Developments after that, such as a replacement for New START, are not included.
  • The ledger is a selection of seven areas. It leaves out many gains and many strains, and it does not weigh one against the other.

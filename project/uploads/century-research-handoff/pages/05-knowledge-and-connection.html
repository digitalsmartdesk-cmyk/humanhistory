The gains

Five entries in two groups. Each gives the scale, the definitions and how solid the figures are.

Learning to read and going to school 2 entries

  1. 1820 to nowLiteracy

    Reading becomes the norm

    Our World in Data says only one in ten people in the world could read and write in 1820 and one in five in 1870, and that more than four in five can now. UNESCO puts adult literacy at about 88% and youth literacy at 93%, up from 87% in 2000. At least 739 million adults still lack basic literacy, almost two-thirds of them women, with 347 million in Central and Southern Asia. Sub-Saharan Africa has the lowest regional rate, 68.5%.

    Definitions
    Historical estimates often count only people who could sign documents, which the page calls a very basic lower bound. Modern figures come from a yes or no question, 'Can you read and write?', which treats literacy as all or nothing.

    How solid The 1820 and 1870 figures have no named source on the page, and its own text says both 'one in ten remain illiterate' and 'more than 4 out of 5 can read', which it does not reconcile. The UNESCO figures come from a news report of UNESCO's estimates, with no single data year given.

  2. 1820 to 2025Schooling

    More children in school, many still not learning

    Fewer than 1 in 5 adults had some basic education in 1820. Now less than 1 in 5 has none. Most children are enrolled in primary school, and girls are almost as likely as boys to be. Around 244 million children and youth were out of school in 2023 on Our World in Data's page, and a June 2025 report of UNESCO's estimate gave 272 million.

    Learning
    In many lower-income countries fewer than 20% of children reach minimum reading proficiency by the end of primary school. Adults in some poorer countries average 2 to 3 years of schooling, about a decade less than in Europe or North America.

    How solid The two out-of-school figures differ and come from different reports and years. The 272 million is a news report's account, and it gave no reference year.

Telephones, broadcasting and the internet 3 entries

  1. 1925 to 2016Broadcast and telephone

    Radio, telephone and television in US homes

    In US households radio went from 10% in 1925 to 50% in 1931 and 90% in 1947. The landline telephone took from 1903 to 1946 to reach half of homes. Colour television went from 10% in 1966 to 90% in 1985, and the internet from 10% in 1993 to a peak of 88% in 2016.

    Detail
    The first chapter gives the full series and the figures for the cell phone.

    How solid From the Our World in Data household-technology chart, read from its data file. It covers the United States only, and I found no world series for these machines.

  2. 1973 to 2024Mobile phones

    A telephone for almost everyone

    The first handheld cellular call was made on 3 April 1973 with a handset weighing about 2 kilograms. The first commercial automated cellular network opened in Japan in 1979, and the first commercially available handheld phone, the DynaTAC 8000x, followed in 1983. Subscriptions passed seven billion in 2014 and reached over 9.1 billion in 2024.

    Smartphones
    Smartphones were about half of mobile phone sales in 2024.

    How solid From Wikipedia. Subscriptions count connections, not people, and the page says the 2024 total is enough for one per person on Earth. It gives no figures for the 1990s or for 2000.

  3. 2005 to 2025Internet

    Half the world, then three-quarters, online

    The share of the world's people online was 16% in 2005, 30% in 2010, 60% in 2020 and 74% in 2025, about 6 billion people, with 2.2 billion still offline. The ITU puts use at 23% in low-income countries against 94% in high-income countries, and at 85% for urban residents against 58% for rural. Africa's share rose from 2% in 2005 to 37% in 2023 while Europe's went from 46% to 91%.

    Quality
    5G covers an estimated 55% of the world's people, 84% in high-income countries and 4% in low-income ones. Access remains unaffordable in around 60% of low- and middle-income countries.

    How solid The 2005 to 2023 figures come from Wikipedia and the 2025 figures from a summary of the ITU's Facts and Figures 2025. Pages disagree for 2023: 63% on Our World in Data and 67% on Wikipedia.

Where the gains thin

These four limits come from the same sources as the entries above.

Basic literacy is not universal

At least 739 million adults lack basic literacy, almost two-thirds of them women. Sub-Saharan Africa's adult rate is 68.5%.

UNESCO, via a news report

Being in school is not the same as learning

In many lower-income countries fewer than 20% of children reach minimum reading proficiency by the end of primary school, and 244 to 272 million children and youth are out of school, depending on the report.

Our World in Data; UNESCO via a news report

Access to the internet is unequal

In 2025 use was 23% in low-income countries against 94% in high-income countries, and 58% in rural areas against 85% in urban areas. Africa trailed Europe by 54 points in 2023.

ITU; Wikipedia

Being connected is not being served well

5G covers 4% of people in low-income countries against 84% in high-income ones, and access is unaffordable in around 60% of low- and middle-income countries.

ITU, via a summary

What the record shows

These are my reading of the entries and my arithmetic, not findings from the sources.

The reach of reading took about two centuries

From about 1 in 10 in 1820 to about 88% of adults now. The early figures have no named source and count a very basic skill, so the starting point is soft.

The reach of the internet took about twenty years

From 16% in 2005 to 74% in 2025. In US homes the internet took nine years to go from 10% to about half, as Chapter 2 shows.

Access and learning are different things

Adult literacy is about 88%, yet in many lower-income countries fewer than 20% of children reach minimum reading proficiency by the end of primary school. Mobile subscriptions exceed the world's population, yet 2.2 billion people are offline.

The gaps are along income and place

Low-income against high-income countries, rural against urban residents, Africa against Europe: in each pair the gap in 2023 or 2025 is between 27 and 71 points, on my subtraction of the figures above.

This chapter measures access, not what people do with it

Whether wider access has made people wiser or less so is a different question, which the plan leaves to Chapter 13.

What this chapter cannot show

  • The measures are literacy, schooling and connections. They say nothing about how much people know, how well they judge what they read, or what they use the connection for.
  • Early literacy estimates rest on signatures and small samples, and the pages that give them name no source.
  • Different pages give different figures for the same year: 63% or 67% online in 2023, and 244 or 272 million children out of school.
  • Mobile subscriptions count connections, not people. I found no count of unique mobile users.
  • Several figures come from news reports of UNESCO and ITU reports, not the reports themselves.
  • The data do not fall on 1825 and 1925. The chapter's series start in 1820, 1925, 1973 and 2005, so the two-century split cannot be tested here.

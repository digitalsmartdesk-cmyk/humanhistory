Evidence brief: the record of the living world

Nature's catastrophes

Sixteen events, from a mass extinction 445 million years ago to the 2019–20 Australian fires

Mass extinctions, a warming event, the loss of the megafauna, collapses of single species and ecosystems, and chemical, industrial and wildfire disasters, in date order. Each entry gives the scale, the cause, what followed and how reliable the numbers are.

The events

Sixteen events in four periods. Each gives the scale, the cause, what followed and how solid the figures are.

Deep time 6 entries

  1. About 445 million years agoMass extinction

    End-Ordovician extinction

    Two pulses of loss ended the Ordovician. One estimate puts the loss at 27 percent of families, 57 percent of genera and 85 percent of species.

    Causes
    The first pulse is linked to cooling and glaciation. A 2020 study instead favoured warming, volcanism and oxygen loss, which conflicts with earlier work. A later proposal links volcanic ash to carbon dioxide reduction, glaciation and anoxia.
    Range
    Genus losses run from 42–46 percent in one estimate to 57 percent in another.

    How solid Contested. The figures depend on the method, the taxonomic unit and the correction for sampling bias, and the page says the Big Five are not sharply defined and sit in a continuum with many smaller events.

  2. 372 and 359 million years agoMass extinction

    Late Devonian extinction

    Two pulses, the Kellwasser and the Hangenberg, together removed about 19 percent of families, about 50 percent of genera and at least 70 percent of species.

    Causes
    Oxygen-poor ocean events, possibly driven by volcanism, weathering by land plants and climate change. Kellwasser may have involved algal blooms fed by river nutrients.
    Range
    Kellwasser genus loss ranges from 16–20 percent in one estimate to 40 percent in another.

    How solid Contested. A supernova has been proposed for the Hangenberg pulse, which the page calls speculative, and the exact mechanisms remain debated.

  3. About 252 million years agoMass extinction

    End-Permian extinction, the Great Dying

    The greatest of the Big Five removed about 81 percent of marine species and about 70 percent of terrestrial vertebrate species. It was also the largest known extinction of insects. The main marine pulse fell within an interval of 60,000 to 100,000 years.

    Cause
    Flood basalt eruptions that formed the Siberian Traps released sulfur dioxide and carbon dioxide, leading to warming, acidified oceans and oxygen-starved, sulfurous seas. Carbon dioxide rose from about 400 to about 2,500 parts per million and temperatures by about 8 °C.
    Recovery
    About 10 million years for most marine communities by some estimates, about 30 million for land vertebrates, and about 75 million for global marine diversity to regain its earlier level.

    How solid Partly settled and partly not. Earlier marine estimates of 90–96 percent were later attributed to confusing this event with an earlier one, plant losses are disputed, and whether land and sea losses were simultaneous is contested.

  4. About 201 million years agoMass extinction

    End-Triassic extinction

    The event removed about 23 percent of families, 48 percent of genera and 70 to 75 percent of species. Marine genus loss is put at 55 percent.

    Causes
    Volcanic activity, oxygen loss and warming. Falls in sea level are also associated with the event.

    How solid Estimates vary with the method: genus loss runs from 37 percent in one estimate to 73 percent in another.

  5. 66 million years agoMass extinction

    End-Cretaceous extinction

    About 75 percent of plant and animal species were lost. It is generally thought that an asteroid 10 to 15 kilometres wide struck the Yucatán Peninsula and caused a lingering impact winter, and a 2010 panel of 41 scientists endorsed that view.

    Lost
    All non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, mosasaurs, plesiosaurs and ammonites, about 60 percent of late-Cretaceous coral genera and about 98 percent of colonial coral species.
    Survived
    Birds, small mammals, more than 80 percent of turtle species and about half of crocodile-group species.
    Recovery
    Forest plant diversity took about 6 million years to return, and lizards and snakes about 10 million.

    How solid The asteroid is widely accepted as the cause, though whether it was the only one is disputed. Volcanism in the Deccan Traps, climate change and sea-level change are proposed as contributing factors.

  6. About 55.8 million years agoWarming event

    Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum

    Global temperature rose by 5 to 8 °C in one estimate and by 3 to 6 °C in another, as an estimated 2,000 to 7,000 gigatons of carbon entered the air and ocean. Benthic foraminifera, small seabed animals, suffered a 35 to 50 percent extinction over about 1,000 years, and seawater pH fell by about 0.46 units.

    Land
    Many mammal lineages shrank in size, palms and broadleaf forests spread into the Arctic, and freshwater animals suffered mass deaths from toxic cyanobacterial blooms.
    Duration
    About 200,000 years, 170,000 or 83,000 depending on the method, with an onset under 20,000 years in the helium-3 record.
    Rate
    The page gives peak carbon injection of 0.3 to 1.7 petagrams a year and notes that present emissions exceed 10 gigatons of carbon a year.

    How solid The age is uncertain, the size of the warming is disputed, and the page says acidification may have contributed to the deep-sea extinctions but that temperature, oxygen loss and food supply are also cited. It was not a mass extinction of the scale of the Big Five.

Ice Age to 1900 3 entries

  1. From about 50,000 years agoExtinction

    Late Pleistocene megafauna

    About 65 percent of megafaunal species worldwide became extinct, 72 percent in North America, 83 percent in South America and 88 percent in Australia. All mammals over 1,000 kilograms were lost in Australia and the Americas.

    When
    Australia and New Guinea from about 50,000 years ago, northern Eurasia between 50,000 and 10,000, North America around 13,800 to 11,400 years ago, and South America after humans arrived about 15,000 years ago.
    Cause
    Debated between human hunting, climate change and a mix of the two. A 2020 study attributed about 96 percent of mammal extinctions over 126,000 years to humans, covering all mammals, not only megafauna. A 2025 review found the literature remained deeply divided.

    How solid Highly contested on cause. Dating is poor in places, especially North America and the Indian subcontinent, and the page uses both 40 and 45 kilograms as the size threshold.

  2. Late 1800s to 1914Extinction

    Passenger pigeon

    The species was once the most abundant bird in North America, numbering around 3 billion and possibly up to 5 billion. A slow decline between about 1800 and 1870 became a rapid one between 1870 and 1890.

    End
    The last confirmed wild bird was shot in Indiana in 1902, and the last captive bird, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on 1 September 1914.
    Causes
    Hunting, which supplied a cheap meat market, together with the loss of the large breeding populations the species needed and widespread deforestation.

    How solid From Wikipedia. The peak population is an estimate, given as 3 billion and possibly up to 5 billion.

  3. 1800sPopulation collapse

    American bison

    The population is put at about 60 million before the 1870s and as low as 541 animals by 1889. The page attributes the fall to commercial hunting and to cattle diseases, and says the killing was part of the genocide of Native Americans, for whom the bison was a central resource.

    Now
    About 31,000 wild bison in 2019 and about 500,000 in total in 2017 including private herds. IUCN counts roughly 15,000 wild, free-range animals, and as few as 12,000 to 15,000 are genetically pure.

    How solid Weak. The 60 million rests on a single observation from 1871, and the page's own low-point numbers conflict, with 541 in 1889 against 325 in 1884 and 395,000 in 1880.

1930 to 1990 4 entries

  1. 1930–1940Soil loss

    Dust Bowl

    Waves of drought in 1934, 1936 and 1939–1940, combined with deep plowing that removed the native grasses, affected 100 million acres, about 400,000 square kilometres, centred on the Texas and Oklahoma panhandles. More than 500,000 Americans were left homeless, and about 3.5 million people left the Plains states between 1930 and 1940.

    Soil
    In many regions more than 75 percent of the topsoil was blown away by the end of the 1930s, a claim the page marks as needing a citation.
    Response
    The Soil Erosion Service was created in 1933 and renamed the Soil Conservation Service in 1935. The Civilian Conservation Corps planted a shelterbelt of more than 200 million trees, and by 1938 blowing soil had been reduced by 65 percent.
    Cost
    By 1940 farmland values in high-erosion counties had fallen 28 percent relative to low-erosion counties, and less than a quarter of the original agricultural losses were recovered in the most eroded areas.

    How solid From Wikipedia. The topsoil figure is uncited there. The page also notes that regular rainfall returned in fall 1939.

  2. 1960 onwardEcosystem loss

    Aral Sea

    Soviet cotton irrigation diverted the two rivers that fed the sea. From 1960 to 1998 its surface area fell by 60 percent and its volume by 80 percent, and by 2007 it was about 10 percent of its original size, split into four lakes. The eastern basin of the South Aral dried up completely in August 2014.

    Life
    Salinity rose from about 10 to about 30 grams a litre by 1990. A fishing industry that employed about 40,000 people and produced a sixth of the Soviet catch ended by 1987, and all native sturgeon species died out.
    Recovery
    Kazakhstan finished a dam in 2005 that separated the North Aral. Its level rose 12 metres by 2008, salinity fell to about 8 grams a litre, and almost 24 freshwater species returned. The South Aral remains largely dry.

    How solid From Wikipedia. The page ranks the lake third-largest in one place and fourth-largest in another, and gives the original area as 68,000 square kilometres.

  3. 1962–1972Chemical poisoning

    DDT and birds of prey

    DDT and its breakdown products caused eggshell thinning and population declines in several North American and European birds of prey. Peregrine falcon, bald eagle, osprey and brown pelican eggshells often cracked before hatching, and DDE is considered a major reason for the bald eagle's decline.

    Response
    Rachel Carson's Silent Spring appeared in 1962. In summer 1972 the US Environmental Protection Agency cancelled most DDT uses, and a federal appeals court upheld the action in 1973.
    After
    The page says the restrictions aided the recovery of endangered species but gives no population figures.

    How solid From Wikipedia. It gives the causal chain and the dates but no counts, so I cannot say by how much any species fell or recovered.

  4. 1985Atmospheric damage

    Antarctic ozone hole

    British Antarctic Survey scientists Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin reported the hole in Nature in May 1985. Chlorine from chlorofluorocarbons destroys ozone, and the cold Antarctic spring greatly enhances the process. 43 nations signed the Montreal Protocol in 1987.

    Size
    The seasonal maximum was 21.2 million square kilometres in 2012, and the hole was one of the largest on record at 26 million square kilometres in September 2023.
    Outlook
    A 2023 UN assessment projects Antarctic ozone to return to 1980 levels around 2066.

    How solid From Wikipedia. The page gives the discovery date as both 1982 and 1985. The treaty and its effect are covered in the chapter on nature defended.

1990 onward 3 entries

  1. 1993 onwardDisease

    Chytrid fungus and amphibians

    The epizootic form of the chytrid fungus disease was first discovered in 1993 in dead and dying frogs in Queensland, Australia. A 2019 review in Science linked it to the decline of at least 501 amphibian species over 50 years, 90 of them confirmed or presumed extinct in the wild and 124 down by more than 90 percent.

    Spread
    The fungus has been detected in 56 of 82 countries and in 516 of 1,240 species tested, though its presence does not always mean disease.

    How solid Contested. A follow-up comment in Science said the 2019 study lacked the evidence for its claims and could not reproduce its conclusions, and the page says it remains unclear how many and which species have been affected.

  2. 2010Industrial accident

    Deepwater Horizon oil spill

    The US government estimated that 4.9 million barrels of oil were discharged over 87 days, a figure BP argued was too high. Eleven workers died. Satellite images showed the spill directly affected about 180,000 square kilometres of ocean.

    Birds
    NOAA estimated between 51,000 and 84,000 killed. Three researchers estimated more than a million coastal birds.
    Other
    NOAA estimated 56,000 to 166,000 juvenile sea turtles killed, up to a 51 percent decline in the Barataria Bay dolphin population, and 2 to 5 trillion newly hatched fish.
    Disputes
    Estimates of the oil that remained ranged from about half to 75 percent, scientists disagreed about the effect of dispersants and about how much microbes broke down the oil, and BP called a 2013 dolphin study inconclusive.

    How solid Estimates differ widely and several are disputed by BP. The page gave 19 September as the date the well was sealed; the 20 April start is general knowledge.

  3. 2019–2020Wildfire

    Australian bushfires

    The fires burned more than 24 million hectares, and the state total on 14 January 2020 was 18.6 million hectares. An estimated three billion terrestrial vertebrates, mostly reptiles, were killed or displaced. Some species may have been driven to extinction, with glossy black cockatoos, Kangaroo Island dunnarts and koalas named as being of concern.

    People
    33 direct deaths and 417 indirect deaths from smoke are listed in the infobox, while the state table totals 34 fatalities.
    Estimate
    University of Sydney ecologists first estimated 480 million mammals, birds and reptiles lost, a figure later expanded to more than a billion.

    How solid The animal figures are estimates that rose sharply, and the page gives no method or confidence range for either. I did not find the page's account of the causes of the fires.

What the record shows

These are my reading of the entries, not findings from the sources.

Every deep-time event has an environmental shock behind it

The six events before humans each have a change in climate or ocean chemistry among their proposed causes: glaciation, oxygen loss, volcanic carbon dioxide, warming, acidification or an impact winter.

Recovery takes millions of years

After the End-Permian, most marine communities took about 10 million years, land vertebrates about 30 million and global marine diversity about 75 million to regain earlier levels. After the asteroid, forests took about 6 million years.

In the human era, the pages name human action for most events

For seven of the ten events since 50,000 years ago, the pages I read name human action among the causes. The megafauna case is debated, and for the chytrid fungus and the 2019–20 fires the pages gave no cause.

Four entries record a response after which the measure improved

Blowing soil fell 65 percent by 1938 after the Dust Bowl response, DDT uses were cancelled in 1972, the Montreal Protocol was signed in 1987, and the North Aral Sea partly refilled after a dam. In each case the cause was one that could be named and acted on.

The numbers are soft

Estimates for the same event differ widely: Permian marine losses were once put at 90–96 percent, Deepwater Horizon bird deaths at 51,000–84,000 or more than a million, and the bison's starting population rests on one observation.

What this record cannot show

  • The list is my selection. Sixteen events cannot cover the record of damage to the living world.
  • The fossil record is biased by what preserves well, so the deep-time shares are estimates that depend on the method and the taxonomic unit.
  • Not covered: the other deep-time events such as the end-Capitanian, the Holocene island extinctions such as the dodo and the moa, and other modern cases such as the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery.
  • Almost every entry rests on a Wikipedia summary, read through a summarizing tool. They need checking against the original studies before use.
  • A record kept after the fact covers only what was measured or preserved, so the earlier entries in particular are the ones that left a trace.
